## 0. The ten rules

1. **Every prop has an owner, a job and a way in.** Before placing anything, name who put it there, what they use it for, and where they stand to use it. If one of the three has no answer, leave it out. (world-rules 3 and check 19)
2. **Props come in working clusters, never alone.** Each place of work has an anchor (a forge, a stall, a mill door, a jetty) and the things its work needs within 2 to 6 cells of it: what comes in, where the work happens, where the result goes. A barrel in open grass is a fault; three barrels against the inn's side wall by the cellar hatch are a story.
3. **Goods leave a trail.** Wherever goods change hands (a quay, a mill's loading door, a goods platform, a market's back lane) there is a staging pile of the right cargo, and the vehicle that carries it stands beside, parked off the walking line. Flour sacks at the mill, fish boxes on the quay, coal at the loco, ore tubs at the mine.
4. **Stores stand against something.** Barrels, crates, sacks, firewood, tools and churns stand against a wall, under an eave, beside a door or a stall, in groups of two or three of different sizes. Never in the middle of open ground. (world-references 7, rule 1)
5. **Doors keep an apron, paths keep their band.** In front of every door a clear patch at least 4 cells wide and 2 deep, on the same tier, with a path or worn ground leading away. Props may flank it, never fill it. Nothing stands in a path's walking band, a stair, a landing or a gate's line.
6. **Everything is made of the place.** A prop's material, wear and colour come from its region and its work: tarred timber and rope at the harbour, brass, brick and soot in Cogsworth, sun-bleached planks in Dust Gulch, basalt and iron in the caldera, concrete, steel and neon in the city, bolted composite in orbit. Wear sits where hands, feet, weather and heat touch.
7. **Busy cores, quiet edges.** Density peaks at doorsteps, stalls and quays and falls off toward the edges of a place, where only nature grows. Every zone keeps one mostly empty node (6 x 6 cells or more) where the companion stands, and open ground between vignettes.
8. **Footprints live on the grid.** Every built prop declares its footprint in 8 px cells, its anchor on the foot line, its collision mask and its apron; its foot sits on a grid line and on one tier. Only vegetation and ground dressing are placed freely. (verticality 4f, rule 4)
9. **Sizes come from people.** Every prop is sized against a 25 px adult (doors 32, counters 13, benches 32 long, crates 11, barrels 14, lamps 56, storeys 32). A prop off the table is redrawn to it in its generator (taller walls, a bigger door, a lower counter), never stretched or squashed as a bitmap.
10. **The world shows time.** Wear, repairs and half-finished work show that people come back each day; seasons and time of day change what is out (washing in the sun, lamps lit at dusk, boats out in the morning). These are scenery only: no rewards, costs or chores (AGENTS.md).

## 1. What the research says

Brief, from developer talks and posts, Game Developer and the official Stardew wiki. URLs are in the source list at the end; the gaps are listed there too.

- **Environmental storytelling is evidence the player puts together.** Harvey Smith and Matthias Worch's GDC 2010 talk "What Happened Here? Environmental Storytelling" [1] defines it as "staging player-space with environmental properties that can be interpreted as a meaningful whole, furthering the narrative of the game". Props are the evidence; their arrangement is the sentence. Smith says the same in his interview on embedding narrative [2]: the player reads an arranged space and infers who lived there and what they did. **For us:** a cluster must read as one act of work (a cart half unloaded at the mill door), not as a list of objects.
- **Cause and effect, and staying inside the world's rules.** Don Carson's "Environmental Storytelling" for Gamasutra (now Game Developer) [3], drawn from theme-park design, asks designers to set the rules of their world and keep them, and to build "cause and effect" vignettes: a scene that shows what happened just before the visitor arrived. **For us:** the world-rules physics (what floats, what stands, where water goes) and the prop rules here are the same contract; one broken rule (a lone barrel, a boat on a lawn) breaks the reading of every prop round it.
- **Consistent rules make a world reasonable.** Nintendo's GDC 2017 talk on Breath of the Wild, "Change and Constant" [4], describes a physics engine and a "chemistry engine" whose rules hold for every object, so players can predict what fire, wind, wood and metal will do ("multiplicative gameplay"). **For us:** our props do not interact, but their look should follow rules the player can learn: wood near heat is charred, iron in the rain streaks with rust, things that float bob and are tied to something, things people carry are small enough to carry.
- **Lived-in towns come from people still living there.** Pixpil on Eastward: "Strong colors, various buildings and objects, rich and subtle details", and "we wanted to make it look lively because people are still living there" [5]; "we handcrafted every single detail" and "elevation is your friend to make an area feel dense!" [6]. Nintendo's interview on how Eastward was made and remade [7] tells the same story of a world built for its people. **For us:** density follows habitation; a lived-in corner has the small objects of a person's day (a kettle on the sill, boots by the door, a bike against the wall).
- **Stardew's farm is a loop of buildings and tools.** On the official wiki: grass cut with the scythe becomes hay stored in the **Silo** [8], fed to animals from the hopper in the coop or barn [9]; things to sell go in the **Shipping Bin** [10]; Clint's **Blacksmith** smelts and upgrades tools and sells ore and coal [11]; Robin's **Carpenter's Shop** sells wood and stone and builds the farm buildings [12]. Each building exists because a step of work needs it, and its props (the hopper, the bin by the house, the furnace) stand where that step happens. Its maps put foliage on a **Front** layer drawn over the player [13]. **For us:** before drawing a place, write its loop (input, work, output, waste) and draw one prop per step, where that step happens.
- **What the games Matt named do** (Observation, `research/world-references.md` 7 and 8): churns, hay and firewood against barn and house walls; washing in the yard between a post and the wall; goods spilling in front of stalls; jars in twos and threes at corners; fences that end at a building, a gate, a post or a face; a well at the junction of the yard path and the field.

## 2. The prop contract: owner, job, way in, footing

A prop may be placed only when all five lines can be filled in. Zone generators should record the first two with each placement (section 9 has the check).

| Question       | Answer must name                                                                                      | Fails when                                                        |
| -------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Owner**      | a resident, a building or a cluster (the smithy, the quay, Moss's stall, the park keeper) or "nature" | nobody in the place would own or use it                           |
| **Job**        | what it is for: work, storage, sale, rest, transport, light, a sign, a boundary, a memory             | it is there to fill space                                         |
| **Way in**     | the cell a person stands in to use it, reachable on foot, on the same tier                            | the stand is in water, behind a wall, on another tier, or blocked |
| **Footing**    | what holds it up (ground, floor, quay, wall bracket, mooring, pole)                                   | it floats, hangs from nothing, straddles a face, a path or a seam |
| **Neighbours** | the cluster it belongs to, and why it stands at that distance and facing                              | it stands alone, faces a wall, or blocks a door, stair or path    |

Nature props (trees, bushes, rocks, ferns, flowers, mushrooms) answer "nature" and follow world-rules 2a (Living things) instead: they grow where moisture, light and soil allow, in clumps, never in a path, and thin out along gradients.

## 3. Places: who lives and works there

For each kind of place: who is there, what they make, use, store, sell and throw away, the cluster that must exist, and where it stands relative to doors, paths, water and light. "Cells" are 8 px grid cells. The props named are ours where they exist; **missing** marks a prop the place needs and nobody has drawn yet (requests for the library, section 10e).

### Farm (the Halt's farm, the headland's fold)

- **People:** the farmer (resident), a hand at harvest. Animals: sheep, a cow or goat, hens.
- **Make:** wheat, cabbages, hay, wool, eggs, milk. **Use:** plough, scythe, pitchfork, wheelbarrow, cart, buckets. **Store:** hay in the barn loft and a stack, grain in sacks, tools on the barn wall, firewood under the house eave. **Sell:** to the mill (grain), the town stall (eggs, vegetables). **Throw away:** straw, a compost heap, broken crates.
- **Cluster:** house, barn and yard form a triangle round a worn earth yard; the well or trough stands between the house door and the animals' gate; the field gate opens off the yard onto the track; the haystack stands at the barn's gable, not in the field.
- **Where:** the barn's big door faces the yard (a cart apron of 6 x 3 cells); the house door faces the track or the yard; fields lie beyond the yard with a hedge or fence, a gate where the track meets it; the compost and the muck heap at the back, downhill of the house.
- **Goods move:** a cart from field to barn to mill on a 24 px track; the cart parks beside the barn, shafts down, never on the track.
- **Wear and time:** a bare fan of earth from each door, tracks at the gate, a patched barn plank in a newer tone, a half-mown strip with the scythe leaning on the fence; the haystack grows through summer, sheep are folded in winter.
- **Never:** a trough with no animals (fault E8), bales standing upright in a mown field, a fence that ends in grass, crops in a square of identical dots. **Missing:** churns, a wheelbarrow, a hen house, a compost heap, a farm cart.

### Harbour (Driftwood Harbour, the isles' landing, the city's docks)

- **People:** the fisher, the lighthouse keeper, the ferryman, the docker. **Make:** catch. **Use:** boats, nets, pots, lines, oars, ropes, ice. **Store:** nets on racks or in a net shed, pots stacked by the slipway, rope in coils on the quay, fish boxes on ice. **Sell:** at the fish stall facing the lane, and up the road to town. **Throw away:** broken pots, net ends, fish heads to gulls.

## 5. Clusters and working triangles

A cluster is the unit of placement. Generators place clusters, then dress them; they do not scatter props.

- **Anchor:** the thing the work happens at (a door, a hearth, a counter, a jetty head, a well). It takes its grid footprint first.
- **Triangle:** most work joins three points: where things come in, where the work happens, where things go out (fuel, hearth, rack; boat, quay, stall; field, barn, mill). Put the three within 2 to 6 cells of each other, so a resident could walk the loop, and let no leg of the triangle cross a path's walking band or a door apron.
- **Dressing:** 3 to 9 small props round the anchor, in groups of 2 or 3, against walls and edges, sizes mixed (a big crate, a small crate, a sack). One small personal object per cluster (a mug on a barrel, boots by a door, a cat on a step) shows the owner.
- **Facing:** people-made things face their use: a bench faces a view or a feature with its front clear for 2 cells; a stall faces its lane; a sign faces the way people come; a boat's bow points to the quay or the open water, never at the land.
- **Distance:** no cluster within 4 cells of another of a different owner unless they share a wall; between clusters open ground.

- **Snap:** a built prop's foot line lies on a grid row; its west edge on a grid column (or its centre on a cell centre for odd widths). Free props jitter 0 to 7 px, but their base cell still checks against paths, faces, stairs and aprons.
- **One tier:** a footprint lies wholly on one tier and never on a face, a stair, a landing or a lip. A prop at a tier's edge stands back 1 cell from the lip unless it is a railing.
- **Stacks and pairs:** a stack is one prop with its own footprint (two crates, a crate on a barrel), so it reads as placed by a person and sorts as one.
- **Hit areas:** a tappable prop declares a hit box of at least 34 x 34 art px at its foot (verticality 5), clear of other hit boxes at standing distance (check 12). True scale never shrinks a hit area.

## 7. Density: busy cores, quiet edges

- **Per zone** (40 x 72 cells): one landmark, one mostly empty node of at least 6 x 6 cells, two to four vignettes (world-rules 3), each an anchor plus 3 to 9 props. In the valley, built props cover about a quarter to a third of the zone's area; in the city the street wall covers most of the frame (verticality 2).
- **Gradient:** inside a vignette, props are dense against the anchor's walls; 4 to 6 cells out, single props (a bench, a sign, a lamp); beyond that, only nature and ground wear. The edges of a zone carry frame plantings, not stores.
- **Never dense where the companion walks:** paths, aprons, landings and the node stay calm and lighter than what blocks the way (art-style, Eastward limits). Detail comes in clusters of two or more pixels.
- **Life per screen:** at most a handful of live elements (animals, steam, a flag), each a finite loop on the shared clock, still with reduced motion, stopped when the screen is inactive.

## 8. Signs of life and time

- **Wear where contact happens:** polished setts at a stall's front and a well's rim, a worn tread in the middle of each stair, grass worn to earth at gates and doors, a dark line on a bench seat, a bright handle on a door, rope burn on a bollard.
- **Repairs:** a new plank in a lighter tone on a weathered fence, a patched roof, a mended net, a slate of a different blue. One per place, not everywhere.
- **Half-finished work:** an axe in the chopping block beside a half-split pile, a hull on trestles, a ladder against a half-painted wall, a cart half loaded at the mill, a hoe left at the end of a row.
- **Weather and material:** moss on shaded lower faces, rust streaks below iron, salt bloom at the harbour, soot above chimneys and forges, sand drifted against west-facing boards in the gulch, snow lying on the lit tops of things on the ridge.
- **Time of day and season** (scenery only, read from the shared clock; no rewards or chores): lamps and windows lit at dusk (`zoneWindows`, the lamp pools); washing out on dry days only; boats away in the morning; stalls covered at night; the woodpile growing in autumn; deck chairs stacked in winter. Each change is a variant of the same footprint, never a new obstacle.

## 9. What never appears

- **Orphans:** a prop with no owner or job (a barrel in a meadow, a crate on a beach, a lone vending machine on a plaza, a cafe table with no cafe, a car with no road).
- **Rows of identical props,** except what people line up: crop rows, fence posts, bollards on a quay, lamps along a street, beehives along a fence, kegs on a pad. Even those vary in small ways.
- **Floating or unsupported things:** nothing hangs in the air unless it flies or hangs from something drawn; nothing stands on water unless it floats or stands on drawn supports.
- **Blocking:** nothing in a door apron, a path's walking band, a stair, a landing, a gate's line, a bench's front or a stall's customer band.
- **Wrong place:** working props far from their work (nets on the open beach, a trough with no animals, dynamite by a fire), furniture outdoors with no reason, signs with no road, lamps on an unlit lane, a boat on grass that is not hauled up for repair, animals outside their fold.
- **Wrong scale or view:** a bench as tall as a person, a door shorter than a resident, side-on vehicles with no top, top-down parasols on side-on carts.

**Checks** (world_lint, report first, then strict per module; Claude A): `prop-owner` (every non-free prop carries a cluster id, and lies within its cluster's radius of the anchor or in a street-furniture band beside a path); `store-against` (crates, barrels, sacks, firewood, churns lie within 1 cell of a wall, a stall, a cart or each other); `apron-clear` (extends `door-shut-in` to the 4 x 2 apron, a bench's front and a stall's customer band); `prop-row` (the existing repeated-props check, with an allow-list of people-made rows); `prop-scale` (verticality 5); `vehicle-on-route` (vehicles stand on rails, water, a road or a declared parking cell).

## 10. The audit (20 Oct, main at 9175f1e)

### a. Method

Every sprite the zones use (the 349 in `assets/zones/props/` that are not animation frames), the 14 garden sprites that are not copies of a zone sprite, the 17 decor items and one front frame of each of the 32 residents: **412 sprites**. A script (kept in the auditor's scratchpad, not committed; the sheets are local only) drew a contact sheet per module at 3x: each sprite on an 8 px grid underlay, its feet on the same line as a 25 px resident, with height ticks at 13 (counter), 25 (adult), 32 (door and storey), 44 (shop storey, eaves), 56 (lamp) and 96 (house to ridge), and its trimmed size and number of placements under it. Door and storey heights were read on the sheets against the ticks (plus or minus 2 px). Every sheet was looked at.

Grades:

- **KEEP:** detailed to the Cainos and Eastward bar, on scale (inside the table's range), lit from the upper left, a footprint that fits the grid as it is.
- **ADJUST:** good art with fixes its generator can make by re-proportioning parts it already draws: a door or storey to the table, walls raised to the eaves height, a simple prop rescaled, a side sliver, a shadow, end and corner pieces, a variant, idle frames.
- **REDRAW:** lacks material detail, is drawn in the wrong view (pure side or pure top), or is so far off the table that its parts must be redesigned (city storeys at a third of scale, a counter at twice the height), so it needs a new drawing.
- **DROP:** no owner or job in the world.

### d. The worst five per owner, and the order of work per lane

Each lane works in this order, one PR per step, with a line-up at 3x beside a resident and the zone at phone scale before it leaves draft (verticality 5, "Order of fixes"). Placement fixes that follow a redraw are Claude E's in the valley and each region owner's elsewhere.
