# Let Terrain Logic Draw NapAttack's World

The 2D top-down worlds that hold together share one habit: they are **real places compressed around a hub**, where a high, cold edge faces a coast, water runs from one to the other, settlements sit at the junctions, and every road, gate and bridge exists because two meaningful places need joining. Players steer by **landmarks and edges far more than by paths**, so designers give each small place one readable silhouette, frame it with edges that are either barriers or deliberate seams, and keep gates soft and loops plentiful. The best in-game maps are deliberately schematic, fill in as you visit, and double as the menu for diegetic fast travel; cosy games enter houses through a quick door swap into an interior that may be larger than its shell, treat furniture as "collect once, place freely", and on phones default to tap-to-move with tap-to-interact. For NapAttack, the research points to a specific conclusion: the generators already enforce border continuity, reachability, region-only props and door clearance (`docs/vision/world-atlas.md`), so the next gain comes from **an explicit semantic model of the world**. That model says what each terrain class means (water has a source and a mouth, anything on water floats or stands on supports, lava runs downhill and crusts at its edge) and lets shading, banks, reeds, desire lines, lamps and corridors be derived from it rather than hand-placed and hand-corrected. The same model becomes the basis for a logic lint that catches the "boat on grass, bench in the pond, bridge to nowhere" class of bug automatically. Evidence quality varies: world-layout and map findings rest on developer interviews and well-maintained wikis, town-layout logic rests mostly on urban-design research and one practitioner guide, and **every numeric threshold for NapAttack below is an inference to be tuned in play-testing, not a sourced figure**.

## Believable worlds compress a real place around one hub

The most consistent pattern across sourced cases is geography borrowed from reality and shrunk. Game Freak built Kanto on Japan's Kantō, Sinnoh on Hokkaido, Unova on New York and Galar on Great Britain, so climate gradients, coasts and mountain spines arrive pre-justified ([Bulbapedia](https://bulbapedia.bulbagarden.net/wiki/Pokemon_world_in_relation_to_the_real_world)). Sinnoh shows the full recipe: **Mount Coronet runs north to south and "splits Sinnoh into two halves"**, the north is snowy and sparse with one major town, the west is rural with a windy canyon fed by a river off the mountain, and attractions cluster in the east ([Bulbapedia: Sinnoh](https://bulbapedia.bulbagarden.net/wiki/Sinnoh)). Masuda's Unova started from a hexagon with "the equivalent of Manhattan in the center" and towns and nature arranged around it ([Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/ds/pokemon-black-white/0/1)); Team Cherry likewise began Hollow Knight with the Forgotten Crossroads as a hub and "everything wrapped around that" ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/uk/how-to-design-a-great-metroidvania-map)). Stardew's Pelican Town is the same idea at village scale: four exits, each to a distinct district, with the Mountain north and the Beach south ([Stardew Valley Wiki](https://stardewvalleywiki.com/Pelican_Town)).

Two details from Team Cherry matter directly to the logical-bug question. They added the Blue Lake **purely to explain where the rain falling on the City comes from**, and they cut a whole area because the world was growing beyond what players could hold in their heads; William Pellen warned that once players move through connected spaces, the world must stay coherent or they lose their sense of place ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/uk/how-to-design-a-great-metroidvania-map)). Coherence, in other words, is treated as a causal property (water comes from somewhere) and a cognitive budget (the map must fit in a head), not as a matter of art polish. Nintendo's Breath of the Wild analysis adds a topographic rule: key locations sit in sunken areas so terrain "gravity" pulls players toward them, and **"important locations are never on a peak or summit"** ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/breath-of-the-wild-open-world-analysis-gravity-to-go-forward)).

### Soft gates, many connections and landmark lures beat a single guide line

Classic Zelda gated with keyed obstacles and took care that they worked "in a proper order without dead-ending the player"; Benjamin Marshalkowski's grid analysis of A Link to the Past's Light World found **only 6 of 256 chunks unreachable**, which he calls pivotal to a world feeling "rich vs. just expansive" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/disciplines/overworld-overload-an-analysis-of-link-to-the-past-s-light-world-part-2)). Modern designers soften this. Andrew Shouldice wanted Tunic to have "as few hard gates as possible", with doors left open "just a crack", and used the camera to hide a passage that players later reach from its other end, expanding their sense of space ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-content-for-no-one-an-interview-with-the-team-behind-tunic)). Pellen wanted "as many connections between areas as we could" ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/uk/how-to-design-a-great-metroidvania-map)), and Silksong builds shortcut doors between distant zones and multiple entrances into hubs so stuck players can explore or circumvent "rather than getting stonewalled" ([GMTK](https://gmtk.substack.com/p/the-world-design-of-hollow-knight)).

The clearest empirical lesson comes from Breath of the Wild's playtests. A "points and lines" plan with events along routes left testers feeling tied to a guide rope, with **about 80% following the route and 20% wandering aimlessly**; Nintendo replaced it with scattered landmarks readable from a distance (glowing shrines, tall campfire smoke, a horse-head stable), each with a clear reward, and used cone-shaped "triangle" terrain to hide what lies behind so places reveal themselves gradually ([GMTK](https://gmtk.substack.com/p/how-nintendo-solved-zeldas-open-world)). Free exploration resulted, yet nearly every player still reached the key locations.

### Seamlessness comes from consistent rules, not the transition effect

Sea of Stars aimed to let players move "seamlessly in and out of water" and over small obstacles, because, as animator Savannah Perron put it, "it really annoys me in games when I can kill a dragon but if there's a fallen tree in the way, I can't just hop over it" ([MobileSyrup](https://mobilesyrup.com/2023/09/11/sea-of-stars-level-design-sabotage-studio-thierry-boulanger-interview/)). Tunic tested fading trees to reveal the player and dropped it because it **"broke the world's solidity"** ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-content-for-no-one-an-interview-with-the-team-behind-tunic)). Between distant regions, transport carries identity: Eastward structures its story as a rail journey east in chapters ([Wikipedia](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastward_(video_game)>)), and Silksong gives the Citadel its own elevator network linked to the rest of Pharloom only through the Grand Bellway ([GMTK](https://gmtk.substack.com/p/the-world-design-of-hollow-knight)). Team Cherry blocked rooms out in 64x64 tiles and tested reachability before art, and abandoned random prefab generation as "a headache" ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/uk/how-to-design-a-great-metroidvania-map)).

## Paths exist because destinations exist

Urban-design research gives the vocabulary that game towns implicitly follow. Kevin Lynch's five elements, as adapted for virtual space, are paths ("channels along which people move"), edges ("boundaries between one region and the next; they may function as barriers or seams"), districts with a common character, nodes ("foci to and from which people travel") and landmarks; crucially, **"the relative placements of the elements are key"** because the elements are "essentially topological" ([MERL TR98-12](https://www.merl.com/publications/docs/TR98-12.pdf)). Rudolph Darken reduced this for large virtual worlds to three rules: divide the world into distinct small places, organise them under a simple principle, and provide frequent directional cues ([CHI '95](https://chi1995.chistatic.hosting.acm.org/proceedings/doctoral/rd_bdy.htm)). A 2025 Wellesley study of players navigating A Short Hike and Sable found that **only landmarks and edges emerged reliably**; paths existed but went unnamed, so players described routes by things like "near the beetle", and NPCs behaved like mobile nodes (the authors call the results preliminary, from three participants) ([Wellesley 2025](https://cs.wellesley.edu/~pmwh/mvmap/papers/open_world_elements/open_world_elements.html)).

Practitioner guidance for Stardew-style maps turns this into placement rules: build a settlement around a centre point, pave where traffic is heaviest and use dirt where it is light, follow a hierarchy from main roads to through streets to local branches, let worn desire paths follow real foot traffic, and **light main roads at night but not side roads or dead ends**, with lamps beside rather than in the path ([Tael's Guide, Stardew Modding Wiki](https://stardewmodding.wiki.gg/wiki/Tael%27s_Guide_to_good_map_making); a community source, not ConcernedApe). Animal Crossing shows where arrival points sit: the original game puts the station on the top edge with the shop and post office beside it, New Leaf runs the railway along the north edge with a crossing into Main Street, and New Horizons fixes the airport on the southern shore ([Nookipedia](https://nookipedia.com/wiki/Town)). In every case the gate is the visible end of a path to a named place, never a gap between arbitrary trees.

### Edges frame each place and water always has somewhere to go

Animal Crossing's town rules are the closest thing in the research to an explicit terrain grammar. Cliffs run across town with ramps for access and "frame the edges"; **every town has a river that feeds the sea**, crossed only at bridges; in New Horizons the river's source is always on the top layer, it falls by waterfalls to the lowest, exits through two mouths, and **never generates narrower than three tiles**; the lowest tier is the largest and most traversable while the upper tiers are kept mostly to pines and flowers ([Nookipedia](https://nookipedia.com/wiki/Town)). Stardew defines whole farm types by their edges and water: a tree-lined border, four cliff-separated quadrants, river channels that reduce farmable space ([Stardew Valley Wiki](https://stardewvalleywiki.com/Farm_Maps)). Players use these edges as references; a Sable dam separating two areas turned up in their directions ([Wellesley 2025](https://cs.wellesley.edu/~pmwh/mvmap/papers/open_world_elements/open_world_elements.html)).

The home plot follows the same logic at small scale. Stardew's farmhouse exterior is identical on every farm map, making it a constant landmark, and the farm reaches town through a connector (the Bus Stop) rather than opening onto it, so **leaving home and arriving in town are separate beats** ([Stardew Valley Wiki: Farm Maps](https://stardewvalleywiki.com/Farm_Maps); [Pelican Town](https://stardewvalleywiki.com/Pelican_Town)).

### Density is built in passes and clustered where people would leave things

An expert New Horizons island designer describes building terrain first, then items, then reshaping the land again with finer detail each pass, and warns against overusing one item or pattern because **asymmetry "can often make things look better"** ([AUTOMATON West](https://automaton-media.com/en/news/20211127-6683/)). Tael's guide adds everyday lived-in props, darker grass under trees, path edges blended into the grass rather than seamed, and a warning that too much detail becomes noise ([Tael's Guide](https://stardewmodding.wiki.gg/wiki/Tael%27s_Guide_to_good_map_making)). Eastward's dense, Kowloon-inspired towns were meant to "seize the players' attention, and then invite them to explore", with the hope that players find "at least one special part of each character and environment" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/disciplines/road-to-the-igf-pixpil-s-i-eastward-i-)). Sea of Stars treated lighting as a constraint on every asset from the start: "all of the assets that we make have to answer to the technical needs of the dynamic lighting" ([MobileSyrup](https://mobilesyrup.com/2023/09/11/sea-of-stars-level-design-sabotage-studio-thierry-boulanger-interview/)). No source in the research gives numeric density targets.

## Maps work best as schematics that fill in when you arrive

Cartographers studying games call the deliberate departure from true terrain **"functional deviance"** and treat it as an expansion of cartography, not a failure; open-world games typically pair a main map with a minimap, about half include a legend, and scale bars are mostly absent ([Morawski and Budke 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42489-025-00200-5)). The spectrum runs from Pokémon's schematic Town Map, where towns are nodes on routes and later generations add zoom, flavour text and live information such as ripe berries ([Bulbapedia](https://bulbapedia.bulbagarden.net/wiki/Town_Map)), through New Horizons' plan that shows buildings, water, bridges and ground but **omits trees, rocks, flowers, items, bugs and fish**, shows unbuilt structures as thin outlines and lists villagers in a sidebar ([Nookipedia: Map](https://nookipedia.com/wiki/Map)), to Terraria's literal tile map ([Terraria Wiki](https://terraria.wiki.gg/wiki/Minimap)). Sword and Shield's map is the most touch-translatable: the cursor snaps to nearby icons, one button recentres on the player, one jumps to the single next objective, and selecting a reachable town calls the free Flying Taxi ([Bulbapedia](https://bulbapedia.bulbagarden.net/wiki/Town_Map)).

Map construction falls into four states (revealed, automap by visiting, triggered by a location or action, and player-drawn), and automaps "encourage exploration to fill out the map" ([Toups Dugas et al. 2019](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10108124)). Hollow Knight layers these into a small economy: a cartographer sells a partial map inside each area, a quill fills in what you explore, and **the map only updates when you rest at a bench** ([Hollow Knight Wiki](https://hollowknight.wiki/w/Map)); pins appear only for places already found, so players choose their level of help ([SUPERJUMP](https://superjumpmagazine.com/getting-lost-by-design-in-hollow-knight)). Breath of the Wild's towers, by contrast, revealed too much at once and pushed players onto linear routes, which is why Nintendo moved to many visible landmarks ([GMTK](https://gmtk.substack.com/p/how-nintendo-solved-zeldas-open-world)).

Fast travel converges on one rule: **travel only to places already reached, from a diegetic node, usually free**. Pokémon's Fly and Flying Taxi, CrossCode's landmark travel and Link's Awakening's warp points all follow it ([Bulbapedia: Flight](https://bulbapedia.bulbagarden.net/wiki/Flight); [Twinfinite](https://twinfinite.net/guides/crosscode-fast-travel/); [Zelda Dungeon Wiki](https://zeldadungeon.net/wiki/Map)). Legends: Arceus allows travel only within an area, so reaching a new area means returning to the hub village ([Bulbapedia: Flight](https://bulbapedia.bulbagarden.net/wiki/Flight)), and Hollow Knight surfaces stag-station pins only after the player has engaged with a station ([Hollow Knight Wiki](https://hollowknight.wiki/w/Map)). The counter-argument deserves weight: Patrick Scott argues instant travel hollows out pacing and setting and encourages large empty spaces, and praises travel that is itself part of the world ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/fast-travel-sucks)). A diegetic vehicle with a short journey scene answers both camps.

## Doors swap scenes, decor is collected once, thumbs tap to move

Interiors come in two families. RuneScape Classic removes roofs automatically when you step inside ([RuneScape Classic Wiki](https://classic.runescape.wiki/w/Roof)), and Richard Garriott insisted Shroud of the Avatar's housing "will not 'teleport' to an indoor instance" ([Ultima Codex](https://ultimacodex.com/2013/03/shroud-of-the-avatar-seamless-building-interior/)). The cosy genre overwhelmingly uses the second family, a door-triggered scene swap where the interior is decoupled from the shell: **Stardew's farmhouse upgrades add a kitchen, rooms and a cellar without changing the exterior footprint** ([Stardew Valley Wiki](https://stardewvalleywiki.com/Carpenter%27s_Shop)), and New Horizons grows an interior from a 4x4 tent to a main room, three side rooms, an upstairs and a basement while the exterior needs only a 5x4 plot ([Nookipedia](https://nookipedia.com/wiki/Player_house)). For camera behaviour across such boundaries, Itay Keren's camera theory recommends region-based anchors and the rule "don't move the camera needlessly" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/scroll-back-the-theory-and-practice-of-cameras-in-side-scrollers)).

Decorating economies split structure from furniture. Stardew and New Horizons put large soft-currency costs on house upgrades (Stardew 10,000g, then 50,000g, then 100,000g for the cellar) while furniture comes from many channels, cannot be sold, and can eventually be had in unlimited free copies from the Furniture Catalogue ([Stardew Valley Wiki: Farmhouse](https://stardewvalleywiki.com/Farmhouse); [Stardew Valley Wiki: Furniture](https://wiki.stardewvalley.net/Furniture)). Relocation is free and instant in both Stardew and Spiritfarer ([Stardew Valley Wiki](https://stardewvalleywiki.com/Carpenter%27s_Shop); [Spiritfarer Wiki](https://spiritfarer.fandom.com/wiki/Boat_Buildings)). Scoring is where designs diverge: New Horizons' HHA awards bonuses but also **deducts for cockroaches, floor trash and items facing walls** ([Nookipedia: HHA](https://nookipedia.com/wiki/HHA)), whereas Unpacking's team left out meters, scores and ratings because observing the player that way "felt strangely invasive for something we wanted to feel peaceful and personal" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/telling-story-someones-belongings-unpacking)). Stardew's green/red placement preview, with bed and fireplace as the only fixed items, is the baseline for placement feedback ([Stardew Valley Wiki: Furniture](https://wiki.stardewvalley.net/Furniture)).

On phones, Stardew's default scheme is **tap-to-move with tap-to-interact**: tap a spot to walk there, tap an NPC to walk up and interact, with the right tool auto-selected; holding a finger makes the farmer follow it but does not route around obstacles, and edge taps can change the selected tool by accident ([Stardew Valley Wiki: Mobile Controls](https://stardewvalleywiki.com/Mobile_Controls)). Pocket Camp uses tap-a-point or hold-and-drag ([Nintendo Life](https://www.nintendolife.com/news/2017/10/feature_our_first_steps_in_animal_crossing_pocket_camp)). Keren's camera guidance adds a camera window that moves only when the player pushes its boundary, eased smoothing and no sudden speed changes, because visual-vestibular conflict causes discomfort even in 2D ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/scroll-back-the-theory-and-practice-of-cameras-in-side-scrollers)). WCAG 2.2's enhanced target size is **44x44 CSS pixels, with larger targets recommended near screen edges and for frequent actions** ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-enhanced.html)), matching the repo's own 44-pixel rule.

## Recommendations: give every pixel a meaning, then derive the detail

Each recommendation is tagged **[S]** when it restates a sourced pattern from the sections above, or **[I]** when it is an inference for NapAttack. Where the repo already does something, that is noted from `docs/vision/world-atlas.md` and `docs/vision/coherence-plan.md`, so the recommendation builds on it rather than replacing it. All pixel figures are [I] starting points for play-testing.

### World structure: a valley with loops and a branching line

The current atlas already matches the sourced hub pattern: the garden sits at the centre of the valley with the ridge north, woodland west, riverside east and town south, and Cogsworth Junction acts as the rail hub from which the west line (Dust Gulch, then the caldera by mine cart) and the city line (Neon Harbour, then Halcyon and Lumen) branch, just as Silksong links the Citadel only through the Grand Bellway **[S]**. Three structural additions follow from the research. First, give the valley **at least two walking loops rather than a tree**: today every valley zone joins only to the hub side, so walking from the riverside to the park or from the halt to the woodland means returning through the garden or town. A riverside path that continues south to the park, or a woodland trail that drops to the halt, would supply the "many connections" Team Cherry prioritised and cut phone walking time **[S pattern, I application]**. Second, keep important quiet places low and busy tall set pieces at the edges, so the garden reads as the sheltered low point and the castle, ridge, lighthouse and halt work as sightlines from it **[S: BotW gravity; I: application]**. Third, keep the valley at its current size; Team Cherry's cut is the precedent for resisting more walkable zones before the existing ones are coherent **[S]**.

The water question the user raised is the clearest worked example. **Being water means three things at once**: the companion cannot stand there; anything drawn there must either float (so it bobs, casts a reflection rather than a ground shadow, and can drift only along flow) or stand on supports that are visibly drawn entering the water; and the water body belongs to a network with a source and a sink **[I]**. Three consequences follow for interaction. A tap on a floating or over-water prop must route the companion to the nearest standing point on a bank, jetty or bridge, never onto the water; that standing point must exist and be reachable, which turns "is the boat interactable?" into a checkable property **[I, extending the repo's 44-px and reachability rules]**. A bench, lamp, crate, tree or resident route may never overlap water unless it sits on a structure. And any bridge must span exactly the water or gap beneath it, with both abutments landing on a path that continues to a node; a bridge whose far end meets grass with no onward path is a bug, as the town-composition notes put it, an edge turned into a seam that leads nowhere **[S: Lynch edges as barrier or seam; I: rule]**.

The valley's water network is the first place to apply this. The atlas says the riverside and woodland reuse the garden's stream function with an x offset, so the stream runs west to east across three zones, and the ridge has a tarn; the atlas does not say where the stream rises or where it reaches the sea. Following Animal Crossing's rule that the river rises on the top tier and leaves through mouths at the sea, the stream should visibly rise in the woodland (a spring or a fall from the ridge's tarn over the bluff) and leave the riverside heading south toward the harbour, either on-screen in a new loop path or as an explicit culvert or fall into the "beyond" forest that the map then shows reaching the coast **[S pattern, I application]**. The same audit applies to every region: the oasis already explains the gulch's dry creek, which is exactly the Hollow Knight Blue Lake standard, and Cinder Springs' hot stream meeting the end of the lava river is another; the city's quay, the canal lock and the Skyport reflecting pool should each declare where their water comes from and goes **[I]**.

### Adjacency rules: build each zone as a slice of its neighbours

Zones should be generated from a shared region model rather than each from a blank canvas, so a zone's edges are consequences of the land around it **[I]**. The research supports four layers of adjacency rule.

**Elevation is monotone and explicit.** Each zone carries an elevation tier map; tiers change only at cliffs, bluffs, slopes or stairs; water flows only from higher to lower tiers, dropping at waterfalls; and the valley's tiers run from ridge (highest) through woodland and garden to town and the harbour at sea level **[S: AC NH three tiers, river falls by waterfalls; I: valley ordering]**.

**Climate grades across space.** Within a region, vegetation and ground change along a gradient rather than at a border: snow gives way to alpine meadow, then conifers, then mixed woodland and grass as the ridge descends; desert hardpan passes through scrub before any grass; lava passes through crust and ash before any plant, with fireweed as the first returning plant, as Cinder Springs already does **[S: Sinnoh's snowy north and fertile west; I: ordered contact lists]**. Encode this as an allowed-contact list per terrain class; a contact not on the list (snow touching sand, lava touching grass, deep water touching lawn) fails the build.

**Edges are continuous and shared.** The repo already paints shared borders from one seed, keeps roads at the same coordinate and width, continues water and bluffs on the same line, and dithers changing ground over the last 56 px **[repo]**. Extend this with two rules: a fence, hedge, wall or treeline that reaches a border must continue in the neighbour or end at a visible terminal (a post, a corner, a gatepost), never be cut by the frame; and **palette and lighting drift across a region** rather than switching at a border, so two neighbours sampled at their shared strip have the same ground tone **[I, from Silksong's one-palette-per-zone readability and Lynch districts]**.

**Districts change at nodes, not at borders.** Following Lynch, a district's character should change at a node (a gate, a bridge, a square), and the zone border should fall inside a stretch of shared character, so the screen edge is never the reason the world changes **[S: Lynch districts and nodes; I: rule]**.

### Transition corridors: narrow, long enough to be a beat, never a tube

The user asked for extended, somewhat narrow pathways between sections. The research supports this from three directions: Stardew's farm reaches town through a connector so leaving and arriving are separate beats; Sinnoh's routes bend before reaching their destinations; and Breath of the Wild and Tunic both use terrain and camera to withhold the next view until the player rounds something **[S]**. A corridor is therefore a deliberate edge-with-a-seam: the land narrows to funnel the player toward the crossing, then opens onto the next place's landmark.

The proposed specification **[I]** is to implement corridors primarily as **shared border bands** rather than as new zones, since the 320x576 grid is fixed: each of the two neighbouring zones contributes a band of roughly 120 to 200 art px next to their shared edge (a quarter to a third of the zone's height for north-south seams), so the combined corridor spans about 240 to 400 px, or roughly half to two-thirds of a screen of continuous scrolling. Inside the band, the walkable width narrows to about two to three times the companion's width but never below the tap-target floor (44 CSS px is about 33 art px at the coherence plan's 1.33 CSS px per art pixel) plus a margin for the companion to pass a resident. Where a transition needs more room, such as the harbour-to-castle cliff path or the ridge trail climbing through the treeline, a dedicated connector zone in the Stardew Bus Stop sense is justified.

What keeps a corridor from feeling like a tube is variety along its length **[I]**. Its walkable width should vary noticeably (for example by a third) rather than run as parallel walls. At least one side should be **permeable for at least half the corridor**, a low wall, fence, hedge with gaps, bank above water or drop to a lower tier, so the player sees beyond it; solid identical treelines on both sides are the tube. The path should bend once, which also delays the next landmark's reveal (Sinnoh, triangle rule). Each corridor gets one pause node (a bench, a signpost, a viewpoint, a stile) so it reads as a place on the way rather than a hallway. The next zone's landmark should appear before the seam, using the repo's existing allowance for a tall prop to rise up to 48 px over a north border. And the corridor's material should be the blend of the two districts, so the ground and edge props change across its length rather than at the seam.

Concrete valley candidates: the garden's south gate lane into Crossroads Town (the town-composition notes already propose a short lane or bridge approach before the first buildings), the ridge trail through the treeline, the halt's west lane, and the harbour-to-castle cliff path with the sea as its permeable side **[I]**.

### Landmark, edge and path hierarchy for every zone

Because players navigate by landmarks and edges rather than named paths, each zone should be specified as a short Lynch inventory before any art: **one landmark** visible from its entry edges and, where possible, from its neighbours; **a frame of edges** on all four sides made of materials that belong there, each either a barrier or a seam with exactly one crossing per path; **one plaza or clearing** kept mostly empty as the zone's node (for the garden this is the quiet companion clearing AGENTS.md already requires); and **at most three path grades**, where grade 1 runs between the zone's exits and its main node and is the only one lit at night, grade 2 branches end at a door, bench, jetty or bridge, and grade 3 is worn desire lines **[S: Lynch, Darken, Wellesley, Tael; I: specific counts]**. Every path must end at a node; every exit must be a break in the frame that a path runs through; a gap with no path reads as a hole and a path into an unbroken frame reads as a dead end **[I]**. Props cluster into two to four vignettes tied to nodes, trees clump to form the frame or shade a vignette, and the walkable band through the middle stays clear **[S: AUTOMATON asymmetry, Tael noise; I: counts]**.

### Detail that follows from game logic instead of manual overrides

The plan to stop hand-correcting details is to make the generators work in the same passes the sourced designers describe, terrain first, then structure, then props, then refinement **[S: AUTOMATON passes; Hollow Knight blockout before art]**, with each pass reading semantic layers rather than coordinates **[I]**.

The layers are: terrain class (above), elevation tier, a water-flow field (direction and source/sink for every water pixel), a **path graph** whose vertices are nodes (doors, gates, bridges, jetties, benches, exits) and whose edges carry a grade, building footprints with door positions and interior features (hearth, windows), the fixed upper-left light vector, and time of day. From these, details are rules, not placements. Banks, beaches and foam come from water-land contact; reeds from still shallow water meeting a bank; waterfalls from flow crossing a tier change; worn grass and desire lines from path-graph traffic between nodes; lamps from grade-1 edges at a fixed spacing and at gates and doors; cast shadows and lit lips from height and the light vector; moss and damp on shaded faces; footprints along snowy paths; puddles in path hollows; chimney smoke from buildings whose interior has a hearth; window glow at night from the windows the repo's helper already records; crust and ash bands from lava contact; and vignette props near nodes, never in corridors **[I; individual rules extend S findings from Tael, AC NH and Sea of Stars' lighting-first constraint]**.

Overrides then become declared exceptions. Each one lives in a data file next to the generator with the reason it exists ("lighthouse on a rock in deep water: supported structure"), the check it exempts, and the zone; a check fails if an override has no reason or if an exempted rule would now pass without it, so stale overrides are removed **[I]**. The aim is that adding a pond to a zone automatically yields its bank, reeds, reflections, the bridge's required path continuation and a blocked walk grid, with no hand edits.

### A protocol for catching visual and logical bugs

Bugs fall into two families, and the protocol should name them so reviews can be specific **[I]**. Visual bugs are seams at borders, palette jumps, shadows that disagree with the upper-left light, z-order errors (a window glowing through a tree, which the repo already handles) and scale mismatches. Logical bugs are physical impossibilities (a ground prop on water, water without a source, lava uphill), access impossibilities (an unreachable door, a bridge to nowhere, a tap target with no standing point), purposeless objects (a gate in no fence, a path ending in grass, a lamp on an unlit lane) and causal contradictions (a dry well beside a full trough, a hearth with no chimney, snow outside the ridge).

The protocol runs in four stages. **Stage one is an automated logic lint** run by the generators and `npm run verify`, built on the semantic layers above, failing the build on any violation of the checklist below. **Stage two is a stitched region render**: every region exported as one image with its zones in place, reviewed for rivers, roads, fences and palette across seams and for sightlines from each entry edge, because Lynch's elements are topological and only reveal problems in relation to one another **[S: MERL; I: practice]**. **Stage three is the "why is this here" pass**: for every prop, a reviewer (human or agent) must be able to say who put it there or how it got there, what it is used for, and how one reaches it, which is the standard the atlas already sets in "the details explain the place" and Eastward's lived-in towns embody **[S: Eastward; I: pass]**. **Stage four is the existing play-test** in AGENTS.md (normal and reduced motion, day and night, interrupted walks, overlapping props), plus walking every corridor and tapping every water-adjacent prop.

### Maps, interiors, decor and controls

For the in-game map, a **two-level schematic** fits the evidence: a one-screen portrait atlas drawn as a railway map, with each region as a painted node on its line, which doubles as the departure board when standing at a station; and a per-region plan in the New Horizons style showing ground, water, buildings, paths and landmarks but no small props **[S patterns; I application]**. Reveal each zone when first visited (NapAttack already saves visited zones), draw unvisited regions as soft outlines in the New Horizons "not yet built" manner rather than black fog, and, borrowing Hollow Knight's bench, ink new discoveries onto the map when the companion naps or returns home, with a cross-fade under reduced motion **[S: AC NH outlines, Hollow Knight bench update; I: nap tie-in]**. Snap selection to nodes, offer a centre-on-me control and one "next thing" marker, show residents in a sidebar, and allow decorative player stamps **[S: Sword/Shield, AC NH, Toups Dugas]**. Keep travel free and diegetic from drawn stations, which the existing `travelRoute` and journey scenes already do; block travel during a nap, mission or battle **[S: Pokémon, Legends: Arceus; repo]**. Any map purchase, per-visit currency or completion reward would change the economy and should be labelled a proposal for Matt under AGENTS.md.

For the nap hut, keep the **door-triggered swap to a drawn interior**, with a short fade or iris, the companion spawned at the interior door facing in, and a static region-anchored camera; keep the exterior footprint fixed as rooms are added so garden props stay reachable **[S: Stardew, AC NH, Keren; I: specifics]**. For decor, use **collect once, place freely**: mission postcards and regional keepsakes unlock items permanently, moving is free and instant, bed and hearth are the fixed anchors, placement shows a valid/invalid preview, and recognition, if any, is positive-only with no deductions **[S: Stardew catalogue and relocation, Unpacking; I: application]**.

For controls, make **tap-to-move with tap-to-interact** the default, with real pathfinding (Stardew's follow mode does not route around obstacles), prop-over-ground priority when the companion stands near a prop, cancellation when the target moves or the player taps elsewhere, and an optional invisible joystick for preference; use a small dead-zone camera window with eased motion, cut under reduced motion, and keep HUD actions clear of the screen's bottom edge **[S: Stardew mobile, Pocket Camp, Keren, WCAG; I: application]**.

### What the research does not settle

No source gives numeric density targets, corridor dimensions, fade durations or evidence on portrait versus landscape for top-down games, so all such figures above are proposals. There is no primary developer statement on how Pelican Town, Pallet Town or other specific towns were planned, and Christopher Alexander's pattern language, though commonly cited for entrance transitions, was not verified in this research. Desktop review and the source harness cannot stand in for phone testing, which the repo already requires to be recorded as missing coverage.

## Conclusion

The research reframes NapAttack's problem. The game's world is no longer short of art or of continuity checks; what it lacks is a declared meaning for its surfaces, from which the art and the checks would both follow. The designers cited here treat coherence as causality (Team Cherry inventing a lake to explain the rain, Animal Crossing forcing every river from a high source to the sea) and treat navigation as a matter of landmarks and edges placed in relation to one another. Putting that causality into the generators, as terrain classes, elevation, flow and a graded path graph, turns "does this boat make sense on the grass?" from a reviewer's judgement into a failing test, and turns detail from a growing list of hand overrides into rules that apply to every new zone, including the train regions that have not yet been built.

The most useful next step is therefore not another round of zone art but a small semantic layer and lint applied first to the valley's water network and its four main transitions (garden gate, ridge trail, halt lane, cliff path). That is the cheapest place to prove the approach, because it touches the user's own examples directly: what water means, what may stand on it, and how one place hands the player to the next.
